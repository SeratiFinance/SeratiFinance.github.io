The fixed parity through which ECB policy reaches 14 African economies.

I study how monetary policy set abroad reaches banks and firms at home. My dissertation examines how European Central Bank easing, including its negative-rate and unconventional phase, transmits through the CFA franc's hard euro peg to bank risk-taking, firms' access to credit, and liquidity and capital deployment in West and Central Africa.

My work sits at the intersection of international monetary economics, banking and corporate finance, using bank-level, firm-level and survey microdata. Alongside the PhD, I have advised on trade and climate finance policy in a pro bono capacity with GIZ.

Lead paper

External Monetary Passthrough under a Hard Peg: ECB Policy and Firm Finance in the CFA Zone

Working paperDraft available on request

How does ECB easing reach firms in an economy that has handed its monetary policy to Frankfurt? Firm-level survey evidence from the CFA franc zone shows that imported easing works mainly through firms' demand for credit and their perception of constraints rather than through wider formal access.

Abstract

Most evidence on global monetary spillovers comes from macro or bank-level data. This paper studies how European Central Bank easing reaches firms through the CFA franc zone's hard euro peg, in a setting of shallow financial markets and concentrated banking. Using harmonised World Bank Enterprise Survey microdata, a difference-in-differences design compares CFA firms with similar firms elsewhere in Sub-Saharan Africa before (2006–2011) and after (2014–2018) the ECB's easing cycle, complemented by CFA-only estimates on the ECB deposit facility rate. Imported easing is associated with a large rise in firms reporting no need for external credit, unchanged application rates and a sharp fall in discouraged borrowers. Overdrafts expand faster than longer-term credit lines, and collateral requirements ease moderately. Transmission therefore runs mainly through internal liquidity and reduced latent credit rationing rather than wider formal access. Turning these gains into lasting credit penetration requires reforms to collateral registries, banking competition and supervisory depth.

Imported Easing and Bank Risk-Taking: ECB Policy, Credit Losses and Provisioning in CFA Zone Banks

Working paperDraft available on request

Does accommodative ECB policy encourage risk-taking by banks in the CFA zone, and do provisions and capital keep pace with the credit risk it creates?

Abstract

The bank risk-taking channel predicts that low policy rates push banks toward riskier borrowers and thinner buffers. This paper tests whether the channel operates across a hard currency peg, where monetary policy is imported rather than set at home. Using audited accounts for 142 commercial banks in the CFA franc zone from 2005 to 2024, linked to the ECB deposit facility rate, I estimate how European Central Bank easing affects credit risk, loan-loss provisioning and capital. Easing is not associated with higher reported non-performing loan ratios, but it is associated with higher realised credit losses and higher impaired loans relative to equity. Net interest margins remain resilient under easing, weakening the profit-squeeze motive for risk-taking. The paper also examines whether provisions and capital ratios keep pace with the accumulated risk. The findings inform prudential supervision by the BCEAO and BEAC in monetary unions whose policy stance is effectively set in Frankfurt.

Deploy or Hoard? ECB Easing and Liquidity, Leverage and Capital Deployment in the CFA Zone

Work in progress

When ECB rates fall, do CFA zone firms invest the liquidity they receive or hold it as a buffer? Firm accounts point to investment, with leverage as the funding channel.

Abstract

When the European Central Bank eases, do firms in the CFA franc zone invest the liquidity they receive or hoard it as a defensive buffer? Using financial statements for CFA zone firms from 2007 to 2024 and firm fixed-effects regressions on the ECB policy rate, I test competing deployment, hoarding and debt-funding hypotheses. Lower ECB rates are associated with higher fixed-asset ratios and lower liquid-asset ratios, consistent with deployment rather than hoarding. Debt ratios rise and debt maturity lengthens with a one-year lag, indicating that leverage acts as the funding channel. Responses are asymmetric: asset adjustments concentrate in tightening years, while leverage responses concentrate in easing years. Large firms drive the liquidity and leverage responses, contrary to the bank-dependence prediction for small firms, and riskier firms borrow more after rate cuts without investing more. The results show how imported easing shapes real activity, not only financial conditions, under a hard peg.
